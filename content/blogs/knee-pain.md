Almost everyone experiences knee pain at some point, whatever their age or job. The knee carries your body weight with every step, so daily wear, sports, injuries and arthritis all show up here. Studies estimate that nearly half of adults will develop symptomatic knee [osteoarthritis](/physiotherapy-for-osteoarthritis) by age 85.

This guide covers the common causes and symptoms of knee pain, how to manage it at home, and when physiotherapy can help.

## Symptoms of Knee Pain

Knee pain ranges from a mild ache to sharp, disabling pain depending on the cause. Common symptoms include:

- Pain when walking, climbing or going down stairs, squatting or getting up from a chair
- Swelling, warmth or redness around the joint
- Stiffness, especially in the morning or after sitting
- A feeling that the knee is unstable or "gives way"
- Clicking, catching or locking
- Difficulty fully bending or straightening the knee
- Limping to avoid putting weight on the leg

## Common Causes of Knee Pain

**Injuries.** Falls, twisting movements and sports can sprain or tear ligaments such as the ACL or [PCL](/physiotherapy-for-pcl-injury), tear the meniscus, or irritate tendons. Fractures and dislocations from accidents need immediate medical attention.

**Osteoarthritis.** Gradual wear of the joint cartilage causes pain, stiffness and reduced movement. It is more common with age, previous knee injury and excess body weight.

**Inflammatory arthritis and gout.** [Rheumatoid arthritis](/physiotherapy-for-rheumatoid-arthritis) and gout (caused by uric acid crystals in the joint) can make the knee hot, swollen and very painful.

**Overuse and muscle imbalance.** Weak thigh and hip muscles, tight calves and poor movement patterns overload the kneecap (patellofemoral pain) and tendons, especially in runners and people who suddenly increase activity.

**Referred and other causes.** A [Baker's cyst](/bakers-cyst-physiotherapy) behind the knee, hip problems and nerve irritation can also cause knee pain.

## Treatment Options

Most knee pain improves with the right combination of load management, exercise and, where needed, medical treatment. Ignoring persistent pain can lead to weaker muscles, stiffness and further injury, so it is worth getting a proper assessment.

### RICE for a Recent Injury

For the first 48–72 hours after a minor strain or flare-up:

- **Rest:** avoid activities that clearly worsen the pain, but keep the knee gently moving.
- **Ice:** apply a cold pack wrapped in a towel for 15–20 minutes every 2–3 hours.
- **Compression:** a light elastic bandage can control swelling; it should never feel tight or cause tingling.
- **Elevation:** rest with the leg raised above heart level to reduce swelling.

For long-standing (chronic) stiffness, gentle heat often feels better than ice.

### Simple Exercises for Knee Pain

Start gently and stop if pain sharply increases.

1. **Quadriceps set:** sit with the leg straight and a rolled towel under the knee. Press the knee down into the towel by tightening the thigh muscle. Hold 5 seconds; repeat 10 times.
2. **Pillow squeeze:** sit on a chair with a pillow between your knees. Squeeze for 5 seconds and relax; repeat 10 times.
3. **Straight-leg raise:** lie on your back with one knee bent. Tighten the thigh of the straight leg and lift it to the height of the bent knee. Lower slowly; repeat 10 times.
4. **Heel slides:** lying on your back, slowly slide your heel towards your buttock to bend the knee, then straighten. Repeat 10–15 times.
5. **Sit-to-stand:** rise from a chair without using your hands, then sit down slowly. Repeat 8–10 times.

### Physiotherapy

A physiotherapist identifies what is driving your pain and builds a programme to strengthen the muscles that support the knee, restore movement and correct how you walk, climb stairs and exercise. Treatment may include manual therapy, progressive strengthening, balance training, taping and pain-relief modalities. Physiotherapy is also central to recovery after [knee replacement](/physiotherapy-after-total-knee-replacement) and [ACL reconstruction](/acl-reconstruction-rehabilitation).

### Medical Treatment

Your doctor may recommend pain-relief medication, injections or, for severe structural damage, surgery. Physiotherapy works alongside all of these.

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek prompt medical care if you cannot put weight on the leg, the knee looks deformed, there is a hot, red, swollen joint with fever, or the knee locks and will not straighten.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When should I worry about knee pain?

See a doctor or physiotherapist if pain lasts more than two weeks, keeps returning, causes swelling, locking or giving way, or followed an injury. Get urgent care if you cannot bear weight or have fever with a swollen knee.

### Is walking good for knee pain?

For most people with osteoarthritis or general knee pain, yes. Regular walking on flat ground keeps the joint mobile and the muscles strong. Shorten the distance or slow down if pain lasts more than an hour or two afterwards.

### Should I use ice or heat on my knee?

Use ice for a new injury or a swollen, warm flare-up in the first 48–72 hours. Use gentle heat for long-standing stiffness. Limit either to 15–20 minutes and protect your skin with a cloth.

### Can physiotherapy help avoid knee surgery?

For many people with osteoarthritis, meniscus problems and some ligament injuries, a structured exercise programme reduces pain and improves function enough to delay or avoid surgery. Your physiotherapist and doctor will advise based on your specific findings.

### How many physiotherapy sessions will I need for knee pain?

Simple overuse problems often settle in 4–6 sessions with a home exercise plan. Arthritis and post-injury rehab usually need several weeks of progressive strengthening.

## Final Thoughts

Knee pain is common, but it is rarely something you just have to live with. Identifying the cause, managing load and strengthening the muscles around the knee make a real difference.
