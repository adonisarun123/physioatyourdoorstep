Post-operative stiffness happens when swelling, pain, limited movement and scar tissue stop a joint from regaining its normal range after surgery. Some stiffness is a normal part of healing. Stiffness that stops improving, or gets worse after the first few weeks, needs prompt attention. Most cases respond to targeted physiotherapy, and early action matters because some medical treatments work best within about 12 weeks of surgery.

This guide explains the causes, how to tell normal stiffness from a problem, the full range of treatment options, and what physiotherapy at home can do.

**Quick answer:** Post-operative stiffness is a loss of joint movement after surgery that limits daily activities. Common causes are swelling, pain-related guarding, too little movement and excess scar tissue (arthrofibrosis). Treatment starts with physiotherapy and pain and swelling control. If progress stalls, a surgeon may consider manipulation under anaesthesia or keyhole surgery to release scar tissue.

## What is post-operative stiffness?

Post-operative stiffness is a reduced range of motion in a joint after surgery that is enough to limit everyday tasks such as walking, climbing stairs, sitting, dressing or reaching. It is most often discussed after knee surgery, including total [knee replacement](/physiotherapy-exercises-after-knee-surgery) and ACL reconstruction, but it can also affect the shoulder, elbow, ankle and other joints.

### What is arthrofibrosis?

Arthrofibrosis is a condition in which the body produces excessive scar tissue in and around a joint after injury or surgery, causing pain, stiffness and restricted motion. It is one cause of post-operative stiffness, but not the only one. There is no single agreed definition, so surgeons usually diagnose it by measuring range of motion and ruling out other causes.

## How common is stiffness after surgery?

Figures vary with the operation and the definition used:

- **Total knee replacement:** a 2021 paper in SICOT-J summarised the literature as about 5 to 6 percent of knee replacements, with some studies reporting up to 20 percent.
- **ACL reconstruction:** a population study found that roughly 2 percent of patients had stiffness that needed a procedure. A 2025 meta-analysis of children and adolescents reported a pooled arthrofibrosis prevalence of about 3.7 percent.

Mild, temporary stiffness is far more common than either figure, because almost everyone feels stiff in the early weeks.

## Why does post-operative stiffness happen?

Several factors usually work together.

### Swelling and inflammation

Surgery triggers swelling inside and around the joint. Fluid takes up space and makes bending uncomfortable, and swelling can also switch off the muscles that move the joint.

### Pain and protective guarding

When movement hurts, muscles tighten to protect the joint and people naturally move less. Fear of damaging the operation can make this worse. Good pain control before exercise is part of preventing stiffness.

### Too little movement

Joints and soft tissues adapt to the positions they spend most time in. A knee kept bent on a pillow, or a shoulder held in a sling longer than necessary, tends to stiffen in that position.

### Excess scar tissue (arthrofibrosis)

Healing always produces some scar tissue. In arthrofibrosis, this process becomes overactive and the tissue thickens and shortens, tethering the joint.

### Other causes the surgeon will rule out

- Infection in the joint.
- Mechanical problems, such as implant size or position after joint replacement, or graft position after ligament surgery.
- A cyclops lesion after ACL reconstruction (a nodule of scar tissue at the front of the knee that blocks straightening).
- Complex regional pain syndrome (a painful condition with swelling, skin and temperature changes).

### Known risk factors

- Limited movement before surgery: research consistently links better pre-operative range of motion with better results.
- Delayed or inconsistent exercise after surgery.
- Poorly controlled pain or swelling.
- Female sex after ACL reconstruction: one population study found women were about 2.5 times more likely to need treatment for arthrofibrosis.
- Previous surgery on the same joint.

## Is stiffness after surgery normal? Normal vs warning signs

| Usually normal | Needs review by your physiotherapist or surgeon |
| :---- | :---- |
| Stiffness in the first days and weeks that improves steadily | Range of motion that stops improving for two weeks or more |
| Morning stiffness that eases with gentle movement | Range that is getting worse, not better |
| Discomfort while stretching that settles after exercise | Pain that is out of proportion, constant or keeps you awake |
| Mild swelling after activity that settles overnight | Persistent heat, redness, increasing swelling or fever |
| Slightly less bend than the other side at 3 months | Inability to fully straighten the knee, or a limp that is not improving |

**Seek urgent care** for a hot, red, swollen joint with fever, wound discharge, or calf pain and swelling (possible DVT, a blood clot in a deep vein). Sudden chest pain or breathlessness is an emergency: call 108 or 112.

## Stiffness after surgery treatment: step by step

Stiffness after surgery treatment usually follows a ladder, starting with the least invasive options.

1. **Prevention from day one:** early movement as allowed by your surgeon, pain control timed before exercise, swelling management and correct resting positions.
2. **Targeted physiotherapy:** more focused range-of-motion work, joint and kneecap mobilisation, muscle activation and strengthening, and walking retraining.
3. **Splints or braces:** some surgeons use extension or flexion devices that apply a low, prolonged stretch.
4. **Manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA):** the surgeon moves the joint while you are anaesthetised to break down [adhesions](/adhesion-in-physiotherapy). After knee replacement, a 2017 systematic review found the best results when MUA was done within 12 weeks of surgery, and most surgeons in a 2021 international survey preferred to do it between 6 and 12 weeks.
5. **Arthroscopic or open release:** keyhole or open surgery to remove scar tissue, usually for persistent or late stiffness.
6. **Revision surgery:** rarely, when there is a mechanical cause such as implant problems. A 2022 review in International Orthopaedics found revision had poorer outcomes and more need for further treatment than MUA or arthroscopic release.

Every procedure in steps 4 to 6 is followed by intensive physiotherapy, because scar tissue can re-form if the joint is not moved regularly.

### What does physiotherapy for post-operative stiffness involve?

Physiotherapy for a stiff joint combines careful loading of the stiff tissue with steps to keep pain and inflammation low. Typical elements include:

- Active and assisted range of motion exercises, done little and often through the day.
- Joint mobilisation and kneecap (patellar) mobilisation by the physiotherapist.
- Low-load, prolonged stretches, such as supported heel props for knee straightening.
- Strength training for the muscles around the joint, especially the quadriceps after knee surgery.
- Static cycling when cleared, which gently moves the knee through a repeated arc.
- Pain and swelling management with ice, elevation and correct timing of prescribed medicines.

More force is not always better. Pushing an inflamed joint into strong pain can increase swelling and set progress back, so a good physiotherapist adjusts the intensity session by session.

## Phase-wise timeline: preventing and treating stiffness after knee surgery

| Phase | Main goals | Typical physiotherapy (examples) | Milestones and decision points |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Weeks 0 to 2 | Control swelling, achieve full straightening, start bending | Quad sets, heel slides, heel props, ankle pumps, kneecap mobilisation | Knee straightening well, bend improving every few days |
| Weeks 2 to 6 | Steady gains in bend, normal walking | Assisted bending, seated slides, static cycling when cleared, gait training | Progress measured weekly; flag any plateau to the surgeon |
| Weeks 6 to 12 | Close the gap or escalate treatment | Focused mobilisation, prolonged stretches, strengthening | If range is clearly behind target, surgeon may discuss MUA in this window |
| Months 3 to 6 | Consolidate range, rebuild strength | Strength and endurance training, functional tasks | Stable range; persistent stiffness may need surgical review |
| After 6 months | Maintain range and function | Independent home programme | Late options (arthroscopic release) considered case by case |

Targets differ by operation and surgeon, so ask your surgeon what range you should expect at each follow-up.

## Can post-operative stiffness be treated at home?

Yes, most of the physiotherapy for it can be done at home. The key ingredients are regular range-of-motion work, mobilisation by a physiotherapist and accurate measurement of progress, none of which needs a clinic. Procedures such as manipulation under anaesthesia must be done in hospital, but the rehabilitation afterwards can continue at home.

Home sessions also make it easier to fit exercise into the day. Stiff joints respond better to frequent short bouts of movement than to one long session.

## Home setup and daily routine for a stiff joint

- Set a routine: short range-of-motion sessions several times a day, as your physiotherapist advises, with a phone reminder.
- Rest the knee straight, not bent over a pillow, unless your surgeon advises otherwise.
- Keep an ice pack, towel roll and a firm chair ready in one place.
- Take prescribed pain relief at the time your doctor or physiotherapist suggests, so exercises are tolerable.
- Keep a simple log of your range of motion if your physiotherapist teaches you to measure it.

**Caregiver role:** help with the exercise routine, encourage movement without forcing the joint, and note any signs of infection or increasing pain. Never push the joint yourself unless the physiotherapist has taught you a specific, safe technique.

## How home physiotherapy for a stiff joint works

1. **Assessment:** range of motion is measured with a goniometer (a joint angle measuring tool), and the physiotherapist checks swelling, strength, walking, your surgical history and possible causes that need a surgeon's review.
2. **Personalised plan:** treatment is matched to the operation, the stage of healing and how irritable the joint is.
3. **Session frequency:** often more frequent while stiffness is being tackled, then reduced as you become independent with your programme.
4. **Progress tracking:** measurements are recorded at each visit, so any plateau is spotted early and reported to your surgeon within the treatment window.

## Key takeaways

- Post-operative stiffness is a loss of joint movement after surgery that limits daily life; arthrofibrosis is excess scar tissue and is one cause.
- Some stiffness is normal early on, but a plateau or worsening range needs review.
- About 5 to 6 percent of knee replacements develop significant stiffness, according to a 2021 literature summary.
- Treatment starts with physiotherapy; MUA after knee replacement works best within about 12 weeks.
- Regular, measured, home-based physiotherapy is effective for most patients.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is stiffness after surgery normal?

Yes, some stiffness is normal after almost any joint surgery, especially in the first few weeks, because of swelling, pain and reduced movement. It should improve steadily with exercise. Stiffness that stops improving for two weeks or more, or gets worse, is not part of normal healing and should be reviewed by your physiotherapist or surgeon.

### How long does stiffness last after knee replacement?

Stiffness usually eases gradually over the first 6 to 12 weeks after knee replacement, and many people notice continued improvement for several months. Morning stiffness or stiffness after sitting can last longer. If your knee bend is clearly behind target at around 6 weeks, tell your surgeon, because some treatments work best within about 12 weeks.

### What is the difference between post-operative stiffness and arthrofibrosis?

Post-operative stiffness describes the problem: a joint that does not move enough after surgery. Arthrofibrosis is one cause of that problem, where excessive scar tissue forms in and around the joint. Stiffness can also come from swelling, pain, too little movement, infection or mechanical issues, so your surgeon will look for the underlying cause.

### Can physiotherapy cure arthrofibrosis?

Physiotherapy is the first-line treatment and improves many cases, particularly when started early. It cannot remove established, thick scar tissue on its own. When progress stalls, surgeons may recommend manipulation under anaesthesia or keyhole release, followed by more physiotherapy. Outcomes vary, so no treatment can guarantee a full return of movement.

### What is manipulation under anaesthesia?

Manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA) is a procedure in which the surgeon gently but firmly bends and straightens the stiff joint while you are anaesthetised, to break up adhesions. It is most often used after knee replacement. Research suggests results are best when it is done within about 12 weeks of surgery, followed promptly by physiotherapy.

### Should I use heat or ice for a stiff joint after surgery?

Ice is generally used in the early weeks to reduce swelling and pain, often for 10 to 15 minutes wrapped in a cloth. Some people find gentle warmth helpful before stretching once the wound has healed and swelling has settled. Never apply heat to a hot, red or swollen joint. Ask your physiotherapist which suits your stage.

### Can I prevent stiffness after surgery?

You can lower the risk. Follow your exercise programme from the start, take pain relief as prescribed so you can move, control swelling, avoid resting the knee bent on a pillow, and attend physiotherapy regularly. Improving movement before planned surgery also helps. Report any plateau early, because timing affects which treatments will work.

## Final Thoughts

Post-operative stiffness is common, frustrating and usually treatable, especially when it is spotted early. The most important steps are moving the joint regularly within your surgeon's limits, controlling pain and swelling, measuring progress and acting quickly if that progress stalls.
